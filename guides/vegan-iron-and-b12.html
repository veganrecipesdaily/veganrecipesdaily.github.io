Vegan Iron & B12

Iron and vitamin B12 are the two nutrients vegans most often worry about, and for good reason: B12 is not found in plant foods, and iron from plants is absorbed less easily than iron from meat. Both are manageable with a little knowledge. This guide explains the best sources, how to improve absorption, and when supplements make sense.

Iron: sources and absorption

Good plant sources of iron include lentils, chickpeas, black beans, tofu, tempeh, spinach, kale, quinoa, oats, and fortified breakfast cereals. A cup of cooked lentils provides about 6.6 milligrams of iron, and a cup of cooked spinach about 6.4 milligrams. The catch is that plant iron, called non-haem iron, is absorbed less efficiently than iron from meat. The fix is vitamin C: eating iron-rich foods with citrus fruit, peppers, tomatoes, or broccoli can double or triple absorption. Avoid drinking tea or coffee with meals, since their tannins reduce iron absorption.

Vitamin B12: why it matters

Vitamin B12 is essential for red blood cells, nerve function, and DNA synthesis. It is made by bacteria and found in animal products, but not reliably in plant foods. A deficiency can cause fatigue, tingling, memory problems, and anaemia, and it can take years to develop because the body stores B12. This is why every vegan should take B12 seriously from day one. Do not rely on seaweed, spirulina, or fermented foods, which contain inactive forms of B12 that do not work in the body.

Supplements and fortified foods

The simplest and most reliable way to get B12 is a supplement. A daily dose of 25 to 100 micrograms of cyanocobalamin, or a weekly dose of 2,500 micrograms, covers most people's needs. Fortified foods such as nutritional yeast, plant milk, and breakfast cereals also provide B12, but you need to eat them consistently every day. For iron, a supplement is usually not needed if you eat a varied diet, but your doctor may recommend one if your levels are low. Get your B12 and iron levels checked once a year, especially in the first few years of a vegan diet.

A day of iron and B12

A practical day might look like this: fortified cereal with soy milk and berries for breakfast, which covers B12 and iron. Lunch is lentil soup with a side of peppers and an orange, giving iron with vitamin C. Dinner is tofu stir-fry with broccoli and quinoa, plus a B12 supplement. Snacks are a handful of pumpkin seeds and a piece of fruit. This kind of day easily meets iron and B12 needs without any special effort, once you know which foods to choose.

Getting your levels checked

A blood test is the only reliable way to know your iron and B12 levels. Ask your doctor for a full blood count, ferritin for iron stores, and serum B12, and get tested once a year, especially in your first few years as a vegan. If your B12 is low, your doctor may recommend a higher dose supplement or injections to restore your levels quickly. If your iron is low, your doctor may recommend a supplement and dietary changes. Do not self-diagnose from symptoms alone, since fatigue and low mood have many causes. A simple blood test gives you certainty, and it is the best way to make sure your vegan diet is working for your health.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all vegans need a B12 supplement?

Yes, or a reliable daily source of fortified foods. B12 is not found in plant foods, and deficiency can cause serious problems over time.

How can I absorb more iron from plants?

Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C — citrus, peppers, tomatoes, or broccoli — and avoid tea or coffee with meals, since they reduce absorption.

What are the signs of B12 deficiency?

Fatigue, pale skin, tingling in hands and feet, memory problems, and a sore tongue. If you notice these, see your doctor and get your levels checked.
