Vegan Meal Planning

Meal planning is the difference between a vegan diet that feels easy and one that feels like a daily struggle. A simple weekly plan saves time, money, and decision fatigue, and it makes sure you eat a varied, balanced diet. This guide gives you a planning system that takes thirty minutes a week and works for any household.

The weekly planning routine

Set aside thirty minutes each week, ideally before your main shop, to plan. Choose seven dinners and five lunches, and check what you already have in the fridge and cupboard. Write a shopping list from the plan, and stick to it. Use a simple template: a curry night, a pasta night, a soup night, a stir-fry night, a roast night, and two flexible nights for leftovers or eating out. Keep a list of your favourite meals on the fridge, so planning is a matter of choosing from the list rather than starting from scratch.

Balancing the week

A balanced week includes a variety of proteins, grains, and vegetables. Aim for legumes at least four times a week, tofu or tempeh twice, and a mix of grains such as rice, quinoa, pasta, and bread. Include leafy greens most days, and a range of colours across the week — orange carrots and squash, red tomatoes and peppers, green broccoli and kale, purple cabbage and beetroot. This variety covers your nutrient needs without counting or tracking. If you notice you have eaten the same three meals all week, add something new next week.

Shopping from the plan

Shop once a week from your list, and buy in bulk where it saves money: rice, oats, lentils, and beans are all cheaper in large bags. Buy vegetables that will last, such as carrots, cabbage, and potatoes, and use delicate greens early in the week. Keep frozen vegetables and berries as a backup for days when fresh food runs out. Check the reduced section for bargains, and freeze anything you will not use in time. A planned shop is almost always cheaper than daily impulse shopping.

Batch cooking and leftovers

Cooking in bulk is the heart of meal planning. Make a double batch of soup, curry, or stew and freeze half. Cook a large pot of grains and a tray of roasted vegetables on Sunday, and use them in different meals through the week. Leftovers are not a failure — they are the plan working. Pack tomorrow's lunch while you cook dinner, so it is ready to grab in the morning. With this system, you cook properly three or four times a week and eat well every day.

Meal planning tools

You do not need special tools to plan meals, but a few things help. A simple notebook or a spreadsheet works well for a weekly plan and shopping list. A whiteboard on the fridge lets the whole household see the plan and add requests. A set of matching containers makes batch cooking and storage easy. A slow cooker or pressure cooker saves time on soups, stews, and beans. A good knife and a large chopping board make vegetable preparation faster. The best tool is a short list of your favourite meals, kept where you can see it, so planning is a matter of choosing from the list rather than starting from scratch every week.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much time does meal planning take?

About thirty minutes a week to plan and write the list. It saves far more time than that in daily decisions and extra shopping trips.

What if I do not like the plan halfway through the week?

Swap meals around, or replace one dinner with a simple backup such as pasta with tomato sauce. A plan is a guide, not a contract.

Can I meal plan for one person?

Yes. Cook in batches and freeze single portions, so you always have homemade meals ready. A plan for one is simpler, not harder.
