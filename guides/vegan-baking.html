Vegan Baking

Vegan baking looks tricky until you learn the swaps. Eggs and butter do specific jobs in a recipe — binding, leavening, moisture, and richness — and each job has a plant-based replacement. This guide explains the science in plain terms and gives you reliable rules for cakes, cookies, and bread.

Replacing eggs

Different recipes need different egg replacements. For binding in muffins and cakes, a flax egg works well: one tablespoon of ground flaxseed mixed with three tablespoons of water, rested for five minutes. Mashed banana adds moisture and sweetness in banana bread and pancakes, at a quarter cup per egg. Unsweetened applesauce works in moist cakes, and aquafaba — the liquid from a tin of chickpeas — whips into meringue for light cakes and mousses. For recipes that need lift, add a teaspoon of baking powder along with your egg replacement, and avoid replacing more than two eggs in a single recipe until you are confident.

Replacing butter and milk

Butter can be replaced with coconut oil, vegetable oil, or vegan butter, usually in the same amount. Coconut oil gives a rich flavour and works well in cookies and cakes, while a neutral oil is better when you do not want a coconut taste. For milk, use soy, oat, or almond milk in the same amount; soy milk is the best all-rounder because it is high in protein and behaves most like dairy milk in baking. To make vegan buttermilk, add a tablespoon of lemon juice or vinegar to a cup of plant milk and let it sit for five minutes — it will curdle slightly and add tang and lift to cakes.

Cakes and cookies

Vegan cakes are often lighter and moister than people expect. Oil-based sponge cakes work beautifully without butter, and chocolate cakes are especially forgiving because cocoa masks any flavour from the swaps. For cookies, chill the dough before baking so it holds its shape, and take cookies out of the oven when they still look slightly soft in the middle; they firm up as they cool. Use dark chocolate chips, check that your sugar is vegan (some white sugar is filtered with bone char), and measure flour carefully — too much flour is the most common cause of dry vegan bakes.

Bread and troubleshooting

Most bread is naturally vegan, since basic bread needs only flour, water, yeast, and salt. Enriched breads that usually contain butter and eggs can be made with oil and plant milk instead. If a vegan bake turns out dense, the usual causes are too little leavening, overmixing, or an oven that is not hot enough — always preheat fully and avoid opening the door in the first two thirds of baking. If a cake sinks in the middle, it was probably underbaked or had too much liquid. Keep notes on each bake so you can adjust next time.

Your first vegan bakes

If you are new to vegan baking, start with the easiest recipes and build your confidence. Banana bread is the best first bake: it is forgiving, it uses mashed banana as the egg replacement, and it is hard to get wrong. Next, try chocolate brownies with flax eggs, which are rich and fudgy. Then try a simple sponge cake with oil and plant milk. Once you are comfortable, try cookies, which need more precision with chilling and timing, and then a cheesecake with a cashew filling. Keep notes on each bake — what worked, what did not, and what you would change — and you will improve quickly. Baking is a skill, and vegan baking is no harder than any other kind once you know the swaps.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best egg replacement for baking?

A flax egg is the best all-rounder for cakes and muffins. For light, airy cakes, whipped aquafaba works better. Mashed banana suits muffins and banana bread.

Can I use any plant milk in baking?

Yes, but soy milk behaves most like dairy milk. Oat milk is good too. Avoid sweetened or flavoured milks unless the recipe calls for them.

Why is my vegan cake dry?

The most common cause is too much flour or too little fat and liquid. Spoon flour into the measuring cup rather than scooping, and do not skip the oil.
